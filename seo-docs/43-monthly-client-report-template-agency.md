Most advice about monthly client reports treats the document as the deliverable. It isn't. The document exists to make a conversation better, and the conversation is what renews the retainer.

Which reframes the whole exercise. If the meeting is the product, then the report is a pre-read, the agenda matters as much as the data, and the action log is the part with the longest half-life.

This template covers the full monthly ritual: what to pull, what to send ahead, how to run the thirty minutes, and what happens after.

## The Monthly Client Meeting Is the Product

Consider what a client actually retains from a monthly cycle. Not the numbers. They remember whether they felt informed, whether their concern got addressed, and whether they left knowing what happens next.

A beautiful report attached to a rambling call produces a worse outcome than a plain report attached to a sharp one. Agencies over-invest in the artifact and under-invest in the ritual, mostly because the artifact is the part that feels like work.

Three consequences worth accepting:

**The report should be readable before the call.** If you're presenting it live for the first time, you've turned a discussion into a recital.

**The call should not narrate the report.** They read it. Spend the time on questions and decisions.

**The action log outlives everything.** Six months from now nobody remembers May's sessions. Everyone remembers whether things you said you'd do got done.

## The Monthly Template

### Pre-Meeting Data Pull Checklist

Run this three to five business days before the call.

```
DATA PULL — [Client] — [Month]

[ ] Wait until the 3rd+ before pulling GA4 (24-72hr processing lag)
[ ] GA4: sessions by channel, MoM + YoY
[ ] GA4: engaged sessions, engagement rate
[ ] GA4: key events by channel — named, not totaled
[ ] GA4: landing page performance
[ ] Search Console: clicks, impressions, CTR, position
[ ] Search Console: queries that moved, CTR opportunities
[ ] Ad platforms: spend, clicks, platform-reported conversions
[ ] Rank tracker: target keyword set
[ ] Check for tracking changes that break comparisons
[ ] Check last month's action log — what shipped, what slipped
```

That last item is the one agencies skip, and it's the one clients notice. Walking into a call without knowing the status of what you promised last month is the fastest way to look disorganized.

The tracking-change check matters too. If key events were reconfigured mid-month, your comparison is broken and you need to say so in the report rather than have a client find the discontinuity.

### The Send-Ahead Pack

Goes out **24 to 48 hours** before the call. Not the morning of.

```
Subject: [Client] — [Month] report + Thursday's agenda

Hi [name],

[Month]'s report is attached. The short version: [one sentence].

Two things I'd like your input on Thursday:
  1. [Decision needed]
  2. [Blocker or approval needed]

Agenda: [link or three bullets]

If anything looks off before then, reply and I'll dig in ahead of
the call.

[Name]
```

Three things this email does. It gives the one-line summary to someone who may not open the attachment. It pre-frames the two decisions, so the client arrives having thought about them. And it invites corrections early, which is much cheaper than handling them live.

### Report Body

Keep the spine short — the depth belongs in the format that matches your scope:

1. **One-line summary** — written last, no jargon
2. **Scorecard** — 5–7 KPIs, MoM and YoY
3. **What happened** — 2–3 paragraphs of actual analysis
4. **Channel detail** — only for channels in scope
5. **Risks** — with your plan and a decision date
6. **Next actions** — owners and dates
7. **Asks** — what you need from the client

### Risks and Asks

Deserves its own block rather than being buried.

```
RISKS
- [Risk]: [what's happening] · [impact, quantified if possible] ·
  [what we're doing] · [decision by date]

ASKS
- [What you need] · [why it's blocking] · [who] · [by when]
```

Quantify risks wherever you can. "Roughly 300 clicks a month at stake" gets resourced. "Rankings slipped a bit" gets nodded at and forgotten.

### Action Log

The highest-value artifact in the whole ritual. One running table, carried forward month to month, never restarted.

| # | Action | Owner | Due | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 24 | Rewrite downtown location page | Agency | Sep 19 | Done | Live Sep 17 |
| 25 | Confirm downtown hours | Client (Dana) | Sep 12 | Open | Chased Sep 15 |
| 26 | FAQ schema on service pages | Agency | Sep 26 | In progress | |

Carrying it forward is what makes it work. Open client-side items stay visible, which resolves the "we were blocked on you" conversation without anyone having to be accusatory about it. Numbering items lets people reference them by number in email.

## A Thirty-Minute Agenda

```
0:00–0:03   Reconnect. How's business? Anything changed on your end?
0:03–0:08   Last month's actions — what shipped, what slipped, why
0:08–0:15   The month: one-line summary, then the 2-3 things that
            actually mattered. Not a tour of the report.
0:15–0:22   Client questions and concerns
0:22–0:28   Next month's plan + decisions needed today
0:28–0:30   Confirm actions and owners out loud
```

**Why this order.** Business context first, because a client whose biggest customer just churned needs different reporting than one who just raised. Actions second, because accountability sets the tone. Results third, briefly. Questions get real time. Plan and decisions close it.

**What to skip.** Reading tables aloud. Explaining methodology unasked. Any channel where nothing happened. Metrics that don't change a decision.

**The confirmation at the end matters.** Saying the actions and owners out loud, then writing them into the log during the call, eliminates most of the "I thought you were doing that" problem.

## Roles

For agencies with more than one person on an account, decide these before the call rather than during it:

**Account manager** — owns the relationship and the meeting. Runs the agenda, handles questions, owns the action log. Should be able to explain every number without the analyst present.

**Analyst** — owns accuracy. Pulls data, builds the report, flags anomalies. Joins calls only when the month has real analytical complexity; otherwise it's expensive attendance.

**Strategist** — owns direction. Usually quarterly rather than monthly, appearing when the plan changes rather than to review numbers.

The common failure: the analyst attends every call and answers everything, so the client bonds with the analyst, and the AM becomes a scheduler. Fine until the analyst leaves.

## After the Meeting

Within 24 hours, while it's fresh:

```
[ ] Update the action log with anything agreed on the call
[ ] Send recap email — decisions, actions, owners, dates
[ ] Calendar-block your own actions immediately
[ ] Log client-side blockers with a chase date
[ ] Note anything about their business that changed
[ ] Update the dashboard or portal if something needs to reflect it
```

The recap email is short and does one job:

```
Thanks [name] — quick recap.

Decided: [decision]
We're doing: [action] by [date]
You're doing: [action] by [date]
Next call: [date]

[Name]
```

Five lines. It creates a written record, removes ambiguity, and gives the client something to forward internally without rewriting anything.

That note about their business is underrated. "Hiring two salespeople in Q4" or "biggest client renewed" is context that makes next month's report sharper and the relationship feel less transactional.

## Running a Live Scorecard During the Call

If the client has an always-on scorecard, open it during the meeting rather than screen-sharing a PDF.

**Why it works.** It reinforces that they can check it themselves, which reduces between-call requests. It demonstrates the numbers are current, not assembled to flatter. And it makes ad hoc questions answerable on the spot.

**When it backfires.** With a client prone to fixating on daily noise, live data invites "why was Tuesday down." If that's your client, present fixed monthly numbers and keep the live view for yourself. More on choosing between these in [metrics dashboard vs client scorecard](/blog/metrics-dashboard-vs-client-scorecard).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should a monthly client meeting be?**
Thirty minutes for most retainers. Sixty only when there's a real decision — budget planning, strategy shift, a quarterly review. Long standing meetings drift into status updates.

**Should I send the report before the meeting?**
Yes, 24 to 48 hours ahead. Presenting it cold turns a discussion into a recital and wastes the time you have with the client's attention.

**What if the client doesn't read the pre-read?**
Some won't. That's what the one-line summary in the email body is for — it reaches them even unopened. Then open the call with the summary rather than assuming they've read it.

**How do I handle a client who wants weekly reporting?**
Ask what decision they're making weekly. Usually the honest answer is none, and they want reassurance — which an always-on dashboard provides better than a weekly report. If there's a genuine weekly decision, report on that one thing, not the full pack.

**What goes in the action log versus the project plan?**
The action log holds what was agreed in the meeting, including client-side items. The project plan holds the full workstream. The log is the shared accountability record; keep it short enough that a client reads it.

**Who should run the client meeting?**
The account manager, with the analyst on call for complex months. If your analyst runs every meeting, your relationship is attached to a person who may not stay.
